02 · Precision: raw data → processed data

The five YIJC PWS02 rules

OperationReporting ruleExample
Add / subtractFewest decimal places of input readings8.8 − 7.5 = 1.3 cm (1 d.p.)
Multiply / divideFewest significant figures of measured input values10.34 ÷ 1.25 = 8.27 (3 s.f.)
Arithmetic meanSame decimal places as the measured readings(20.8+20.7)/2 = 20.75 → 20.8 s (1 d.p.)
log₁₀ of quantityNumber of decimal places in log matches s.f. in the inputlg(23.4) = 1.369 (3 d.p.)
sin/cos/tanResult in same number of s.f. as inputsin 53° ≈ 0.80 (2 s.f.)
Exact counts are not limiting measurements. N=20 oscillations, factor 2 for an average, π and cm→m conversion factors do not automatically force 1 or 2 s.f. Do not confuse “N=20” with a measured quantity stated only to 1 s.f.

Retain a guard digit or full calculator precision during intermediate calculations, then round displayed calculated columns appropriately. When computing repeated timings, use the exact mean before rounding the final period. Never add fictitious raw readings.

Apparatus precision: record what the instrument genuinely resolves

InstrumentTypical reporting conventionRemarks
Metre rule / ruler0.1 cm (1 mm)Read eye square-on; avoid parallax; values such as 15.0 cm, 7.5 cm.
Micrometer0.01 mmZero check, use ratchet, measure in more than one place/orientation.
Vernier calipers0.01 cm (typical)Check zero error, line up jaws correctly.
Digital stopwatchDisplayed resolution, often 0.01 sReaction-time uncertainty can dominate; the DHS solution also accepts timing to 0.1 s.
Digital ammeter/multimeterOne digit in last displayed placeEg 140.6 mA = 0.1406 A.

Repeat versus average: Repeating a reading can reduce scatter and may earn procedural marks, but repeating everything is not an automatic solution to systematic error. Follow apparatus availability and the question's demand. If repeat readings are obtained to 2 d.p., their mean is recorded to 2 d.p.; a final result may legitimately carry more s.f. than a single reading if its magnitude warrants it.

Two hazards with zeros

  • Zero as an experimental data point: For an unladen rubber band, enter m=0.000 kg, e=0.000 m, F=0.000 N at the precision of each column, not inconsistent bare “0”.
  • 0 d.p. does not necessarily mean one significant figure: L=194 cm has 0 d.p. but three reported significant digits. Record estimated tail lengths in the manner specified by the scheme.

03 · Measurement uncertainties and conclusions

Work with absolute and fractional uncertainties separately

Addition or subtraction

z = a ± b → Δz = Δa + Δb

Add absolute uncertainties even for subtraction. If a constant count multiplies a length, it also multiplies its absolute uncertainty.

Multiplication or division

z = ab/c → Δz/z ≈ Δa/a + Δb/b + Δc/c

Add fractional or percentage uncertainties, with an exponent multiplying the fractional uncertainty.

z = aⁿ → (Δz/z) ≈ |n|(Δa/a)

DHS 3(b): a mixed calculation

Lᵧ = 13πDᵧ + 2Ltail = 13π(4.5) + 2(5) ≈ 194 cm
ΔLᵧ = 13πΔDᵧ + 2ΔLtail
%ΔLᵧ = [13π(0.2)+2(0.2)] / 194 × 100% ≈ 4.4%

This 4.4% is the DHS key's value, based on its assumed absolute uncertainties of 0.2 cm for diameter and a tail. Do not substitute the digital precision of a micrometer or infer these uncertainty values from the mock alone.

Checking whether a constant is genuinely constant

Compute k from two data sets. Compare the percentage difference in k to the relevant percentage uncertainty in the experiment. If difference exceeds uncertainty, the proposed relationship is not supported by those results.

% difference = |k₁ − k₂| / min(k₁,k₂) × 100%

The YIJC worksheet recommends the smaller-value denominator. DHS 2024 additionally accepts an average-value denominator when applied with a coherent definition. For its example, kᵧ≈4.73×10⁻⁷ Ω m and k𝓏≈3.51×10⁻⁷ Ω m, giving about 35% (smaller denominator) versus 4.4% uncertainty in Lᵧ. It does not support the suggested relation for that data.

04 · Tables, graphing and line analysis

Data-table standard

  1. Columns ordered: independent variable → raw dependent readings (and repeats) → mean → processed variables.
  2. Headings include quantity / unit (x/cm, t₁/s, IR/V); logs should use lg(f/Hz) rather than log of an unlabelled dimensional number.
  3. Border all rows/columns with straight rules. At least the required number of valid data sets and suitable range.
  4. Consistent d.p. for raw readings, and appropriate s.f. or d.p. for derived columns. Keep workings outside the table.

Best fit—not dot-to-dot

  1. Derive which variable goes on each axis from the given linearised equation.
  2. Scale sensibly so the data occupy much of the grid; DHS expects increments 1, 2 or 5 (or 10×) per large division.
  3. Plot measured values to half of the smallest graph-paper division where possible.
  4. Use a single straight best-fit line where linear; if non-linear, draw a smooth best-fit curve. Do not join adjacent dots.
  5. Circle/label a genuinely anomalous point without crossing out the raw entry. Only exclude from the line if there is a justified trend-based reason.
  6. For a gradient, select two far-apart points on the best-fit line, not arbitrary raw data points. Show the large triangle, coordinates, working, units and final precision.
  7. Where extrapolation is appropriate, calculate intercept algebraically as c=y−mx; a distant graphical y-intercept can be inaccurate.
Least-squares vs hand graph: A spreadsheet regression is useful as a check, but the handwritten DHS solutions mark a graph-derived large-triangle gradient. Numerical differences from a least-squares regression do not alone mean an answer is wrong.

Units by homogeneity

T⁴ = Px + Q; [T⁴]=s⁴; [x]=cm → [P]=s⁴ cm⁻¹; [Q]=s⁴
IR=GR+H; [IR]=A Ω=V → [G]=A; [H]=V; [H/G]=Ω

Units follow the actual axis units, not whichever units seem conventional. If x is in metres, the numerical P and its unit both change.

Do not approximate a visibly curved force–extension graph by a single origin-to-endpoint triangle. The key uses trapezoidal regions and explicitly warns against that underestimate.

Comparing an ohmmeter reading and calculated coil resistance

DHS provides multimeter reading 21.6 Ω for Z, higher than the graph-derived 12.1 Ω. Acceptable scheme explanations include contact resistance at the crocodile clips that contributes to direct resistance measurement, or coil heating that increases metal resistance during a measurement. Be specific about the apparatus and direction of the discrepancy. Avoid inventing disconnected or exposed wire ends.

Evaluate k and decide whether relation holds

X = kL/d² → k=Xd²/L
kᵧ≈4.7×10⁻⁷ Ω m; k𝓏≈3.5×10⁻⁷ Ω m
% difference ≈ 35% (DHS smaller-value denominator) > 4.4% → not supported

Magnetic field comparison: use variables in the formula!

B=CnI,   n=N/l

Y has both greater current I (from experiment / graph) and more turns per unit length n (from tube observations). Therefore Bᵧ > B𝓏, with both supplied the same voltage in the comparison. Thinner Z wire alone is insufficient evidence for the opposite result.

Compass verification

  1. Connect the battery directly across coil Y with a switch. Place coil along east–west; locate a compass at fixed distance from one end.
  2. With switch open, the needle points toward magnetic north. Close switch and measure deflection angle with a protractor.
  3. Repeat for Z keeping compass distance, orientation and supply conditions fixed. Larger deflection angle indicates stronger field under the same ambient geomagnetic field conditions.

08 · Writing a 12-mark physics planning answer

Combine EJC's structure (setup, identification of variables, apparatus and procedure, analysis, safety/precision) with DHS's experiment-specific scoring. Avoid filler: planning marks are attached to concrete measurable actions and correctly derived graph tests.

Planning response template

  1. Purpose + IV, DV, CV: state each with symbol and how CV is held constant.
  2. Fully labelled setup: stands, clamps, bench line, sensor placement, distances, power/circuit.
  3. Measure: name apparatus, quantity measured, exact readout/calculation method.
  4. Experiment A: 6+ suitable levels of IV while CV fixed; repeats if meaningful.
  5. Experiment B: if a second IV is required, swap which factor is fixed.
  6. Process/analyse: full linearization, axes, gradients, intercepts, conclusion.
  7. Improvements: 2–4 specific measures linked to a known uncertainty or limitation.
  8. Safety: only where relevant/requested, realistic equipment-specific risk controls.

DHS Q4—the two-IV example

f=kρˣLʸ
lg f = lg k + x lg ρ + y lg L

Experiment 1: rods of the same material/density, different lengths. Plot lg(f/Hz) vs lg(L/m); gradient = y.

Experiment 2: different metal densities, same rod length. Plot lg(f/Hz) vs lg(ρ/[kg m⁻³]); gradient = x.

Use enough levels, repeat the measured frequency and ensure rods are secured at their midpoint.

DHS Q4: workable instrument method

  1. Clamp the solid thin rod firmly at its centre; place microphone near the opposite, non-struck end and connect to calibrated CRO.
  2. Strike axially at one end (preferably a slotted mass released in a controlled way from a fixed height to keep the impulse similar).
  3. On CRO, measure the width in divisions of a complete periodic waveform and multiply by time-base (s per division). f=1/T. Adjust time-base and microphone placement so a measurable signal is captured.
  4. Measure rod length L with ruler, rod mass m with balance, diameter d with micrometer; for a solid cylinder: ρ=4m/(πd²L)
  5. Check the mode/fundamental is appropriate; the scheme suggests comparing measured f to vsound in metal/(2L) if speed information is available.

Specific improvements the DHS key rewards

  • Measure d at multiple positions around and along the rod and average.
  • Use a quiet room to reduce acoustic background; an anechoic space if needed to avoid reverberation.
  • Place the microphone appropriately or use a sound-collecting cone for adequate received signal.
  • Adjust CRO time-base to show clearly one full waveform.
  • Use sufficiently long rods to reduce the relative uncertainty in L.
  • Do preliminary trials to choose an appropriate rod L/density range for an adequately detectable frequency.
  • When changing metal, cut or select rods of the same length and verify with metre rule; keep room temperature controlled if this affects the measurement.
The DHS examiner notes caution that a stroboscope or camera might detect transverse motion, not reliably the intended longitudinal stationary sound-wave frequency. Prefer microphone + CRO with a clear time-base method. A generic mention of FFT/specialised software without measurement details is weak.

09 · Sources of error, precautions and improvements

From the consolidated practical notes: give a specific physical cause → what measurement is affected (and how) → an improvement that targets the cause. Don't answer merely “human error,” “parallax,” “repeat measurements” or “use precise instruments” without a reason tied to the setup.

ExperimentMechanism and effectTargeted improvement
Pendulum / timingReaction delay is a larger fraction of one short oscillation, producing timing scatter.Time 20–25 oscillations; repeat and average times; for higher accuracy use light gate at suitable position.
Length measurementEye not perpendicular to rule generates a displaced apparent position.Position eye normal to scale; use pointer/set square where possible.
Rubber-band extensionMass hanger swinging makes apparent L unstable; rubber band may exhibit hysteresis and creep.Wait for oscillations to die down; define when reading is taken; approach loads consistently.
Wire diameterNonuniform thickness biases calculated cross-sectional area, amplified by d² dependence.Micrometer readings at several locations and rotations; average; check zero error.
Coil currentResistance changes with wire temperature if current heats conductor.Close switch briefly, wait appropriate settling; minimise heating and keep timing consistent.
Coil direct ΩCrocodile-clip contact resistance may contribute to the direct DMM resistance measurement.Clean/sufficiently tighten contacts, or use suitable lead-compensation method; explain measurement configuration.
Rod resonanceUncontrolled strike excites different amplitudes/modes; reflection/reverberation corrupts the waveform.Repeat controlled axial impacts; clamp exactly in middle; quiet room; correctly positioned microphone and CRO.

Random errors give scatter and often benefit from repeat/average and broad measurement range. Systematic errors bias readings and demand calibration, offset correction or changed technique; repeated measurements alone do not remove them.

10 · Cross-paper and extra-source lessons

From EJC planning notes and 2018 practice set

  • Begin with the exact question objective. If asked for exponents x and y, do not spend all analysis determining an unrequested k. If asked to verify a relation, give the line/shape predicted by the relation.
  • Unknown relationship: choose a physically sensible model, often y=kxⁿ; lg y = lg k + n lg x, then test it. For attenuation, an exponential law and ln transform may be appropriate.
  • Sensor + data logger: naming a computer alone is not a measurement method. State sensor, position, time sampling and what is calculated.
  • Choose physical apparatus that directly measures the variable: sound sensor/CRO for audio frequency; light gate for brief moving-object times; micrometer for thin wire; Hall probe for magnetic flux density.
  • Control variables by action: e.g., same liquid column depth, same source-to-detector distance, fixed supply voltage, common initial temperature, same rod length—do not merely say “all others constant.”
  • Log plots: correctly account for units in axis heading: lg(L/m), lg(f/Hz), lg(ρ/(kg m⁻³)), with gradient dimensionless for a ratio of log variables.
  • Detailed setup drawing: show bench, supports, clamps, alignment, electrical connections, source/receiver and dimensions; avoid floating equipment.

Easy-to-miss scoring details from the DHS marking scheme

  • A complete table must include repeated raw readings when the experimental method requires them; 1(c) specifically asks six datasets and a span ≥10 cm.
  • Q1 timing must be at least 10 s and should cover multiple oscillations; a value of “20T” written as time without actual measurement is not accepted.
  • DHS Q2: e=0 when m=0 must appear in the table with precision. Five nonzero datasets are still required.
  • DHS Q3: don't use unprovided 50 g increments for masses if the apparatus only has certain values (context-specific).
  • DHS Q3: for B=CnI, compare n and I instead of relying on wire thickness alone.
  • DHS Q4: improvements are more useful than unrelated safety paragraphs; the examiner specifically notes that generic safety points were often not credited in that planning question.

11 · Practical exam checklist

Before measuring

  • Underline every quantity and identify the physical meaning of symbols (t vs T; N vs n).
  • Check required instrument and resolution/units.
  • Plan repetitions, range and 5–6+ data points.
  • Check zero/calibration and apparatus alignment.

While recording

  • Keep all raw readings with consistent decimal places.
  • Record repeats separately, not just their average.
  • Never invent a second reading from a provided one.
  • Log exact counts and relevant control variables.

For processing and graphs

  • Mean → derived variable; apply correct dp/sf per operation.
  • Include units, including zero rows and processed columns.
  • Plot all valid points on sensible axes; fit curve/line.
  • Use large triangle; state gradient, intercept and units.
  • Interpret discrepancies relative to uncertainty, not intuition.

For planning

  • Label apparatus diagram, clamps, bench and sensor.
  • IV/DV/CVs with concrete control methods.
  • Two experiments if two independent exponents.
  • Write exactly how frequency/quantity is measured.
  • Graph linearisation + gradients + required constants.
  • Add relevant and justified accuracy improvements.

Full-mark diagram requirements

Draw the benchtop; support devices with retort stands, bosses and clamps; show required geometry with arrows; use separate circuit/layout diagrams as needed. Avoid floating equipment or unlabeled blocks. Include the actual measurable dimensions and orientation, not decorative sketches.

Computers, loggers and mini-planning

EJC warns that saying “use a computer” is insufficient: specify sensor, acquisition timing, reference calibration and processing. Data loggers can automatically take successive readings, record very rapid events, and synchronise different sensors. For mini-planning after a main experiment, describe exactly what is changed, controlled and tested, and any physical limit on the number of valid values.

Uncertainty and error distinctions

Random: scatter that can be reduced by repeated trials and better resolution. Systematic: bias that repeats do not remove. Analogue: typically half the smallest division unless judgement justifies a full division. Digital: typically ±1 least displayed digit. Explicitly name the measured quantity in every evaluation.

17 · Further planning models and precision traps

Two-variable power law (teacher planning notes, pp.7–16)

C = k dM tN

Run 1 at fixed t: plot lg C against lg d; gradient M. Run 2 at fixed d: plot lg C against lg t; gradient N. In a GM-tube setup measure d with a rule, detector-plate thickness t with micrometer/calipers, and count rate with GM tube plus ratemeter. Keep the source activity, detector alignment and measurement window controlled. Subtract background counts and use tongs for the source.

Practice response format

  1. Purpose/variables: state IV/DV/CVs using symbols and explicit physical quantities.
  2. Diagram: workable labelled setup on bench; include power/sensor connections.
  3. Procedures: concrete instrument, first reading, range of 6 values (or more as suitable), second controlled sweep if needed, repeat and average a named reading.
  4. Analysis: full algebra of linearisation, chosen axes with unit-free logarithms formed from quantity/unit ratios, meaning of each gradient/intercept.
  5. Accuracy: 2–3 feasible, setup-specific refinements; remove meaningful background/offset and verify geometry.
  6. Safety: only if relevant and explicitly rewarded; state action linked to hazard.

Graphing traps from YIJC PWS02

  • Label a log column as lg(t/s), not as “lg t / s”.
  • Keep every recorded raw datum even when a point is anomalous; mark excluded points but do not erase them.
  • Do not assume every graph is linear: rubber-band force-extension can require a smooth curve; a verified linear relationship calls for a best-fit straight line.
  • Choose axes, major intervals and plot size to exploit grid area; read coordinates from the line, not necessarily a raw dot.
  • For two constants compare the percentage difference against the relevant stated percentage uncertainty; note DHS permits multiple explicitly defined denominator conventions.
  • For dimensional homogeneity: units of gradient = (y-axis unit)/(x-axis unit), units of intercept = y-axis unit.

19 · Mechanics, equilibrium and material properties

Core physical ideas: extension = final − original length; Hooke's law within the proportional region; Young modulus = stress/strain within the relevant elastic regime; moment = force × perpendicular distance; constant-speed motion means zero resultant force; terminal speed occurs when resistance balances the driving force.

Measurements and targeted improvements

Use a metre rule for large lengths and a micrometer for thin diameters; inspect zero error and repeat nonuniform measurements in two perpendicular orientations. Use a force sensor or stable newton meter after zeroing. Wait for an extension to settle; control load application and temperature. For falling objects, choose light gates and appropriately narrow flags instead of timing a short fall by hand. If the model requires small elastic strains, limit the applied force to the proportional regime. Unit guard: N·m = J, N·m⁻¹ is stiffness, and Pa = N·m⁻².

Correct timed-cycle workflow (critical DHS lesson)

  1. Choose N so the timed interval is sufficiently long; in DHS, repeated readings and t ≥ 10.0 s were explicitly rewarded.
  2. Time N complete oscillations twice (t₁ and t₂); keep N clearly recorded for each row because it can vary between rows.
  3. Calculate t̄ = (t₁ + t₂)/2, then T = t̄/N. N is an exact count, not a measured quantity that restricts significant figures.
  4. Apply the stated raw timing d.p., mean d.p., and processed T s.f.; calculate T⁴ from guard-digit values when plotting, and correctly label x units.
  5. Release from small consistent amplitude without a push; measure pivot-to-bob centre for simple pendulum lengths and keep coupled-system geometry fixed.

Error mechanism: hand timing has reaction-time contribution independent of the number of cycles; timing many cycles reduces its fraction of the total. A large starting angle can lengthen the measured simple-pendulum period. Damping reduces amplitude; it is not by itself proof that the natural frequency changes substantially.

Methods: insulated vessel and lid reduce energy losses; stir to minimise temperature gradients; do not press a temperature probe against the container; wait for thermal equilibrium when appropriate. Exam-grade graph production

  1. Transcribe all original data with correct units and repeat columns; do not hide or overwrite anomalies.
  2. Linearise first. Label both axes variable/unit or use scaled units, e.g. T⁴/s⁴ versus x/cm.
  3. Use sensible 1–2–5 major scale progression, plotted crosses/dots distinguishable from fit, and ≥half-smallest-division plotting precision where required.
  4. Draw best-fit line or a smooth curve as physically warranted; only omit anomalies from the fit with defensible justification, retain original marked points.
  5. Use a large gradient triangle on the fit, not arbitrary original data points; quote coordinates and slope units. Intercept can be obtained by substitute a point on the line.
  6. For area, integrate only over the physically requested interval and use rectangles/trapezia accurately when curve is nonlinear.

Comparison of constants

% difference (DHS-accepted options) = |k₁−k₂|/ksmaller ×100%   OR   |k₁−k₂|/kaverage ×100%

State which convention is used, then compare against the relevant combined percentage uncertainty. A discrepancy significantly greater than uncertainty means the measurements do not support the proposed relationship. If the problem provides uncertainty only for one term, follow that comparison instruction rather than quietly inventing uncertainties.

Source-of-error writing formula

Cause → effect on the measured quantity/result (direction) → specific improvement → physical reason the improvement helps. Repetition addresses random errors, not systematic biases. Calibration addresses offset; thermal and geometrical controls address experiment-specific systematic deviations.
